Design Concept

I wanted to turn this solid building into a transparent, breathing, strange snow globe.

The Vision

I chose projection mapping because I wanted to do something magical. A snow globe is like a tiny world you can hold — full of snow and memories. My goal was to make everyone who saw this projection feel that pure, dreamy winter feeling instantly.

I wanted to make the building a poetic landmark. Not a billboard. Not a display surface. A place that temporarily transcended its physical form to hold an entire winter inside.

The Core Question

The power of projection mapping lies not in illuminating space, but in reinventing it. We weren't merely illuminating walls — we were rewriting the building's identity, breathing a new soul into it.

Through design, a solid, physical, three-dimensional object can be imbued with a fourth dimension: emotion and narrative. This project was the proof of that idea.

The Central Problem

The fight between
reality and imagination.

My "model" was just a completely still photo of a building. But I wanted to create a feeling of something flowing and alive inside a snow globe.

This created a huge problem: how could I make this flat picture feel deep? The moving water ripples had to flow across the building's surface. The snow had to fall from inside it — not just float in front like a sticker.

The Constraint

Making these elements grow from the building — not stick on it — was the biggest challenge.

Challenges & Solutions

Two problems.
Two precise fixes.

01
Corner Anchoring — The Feature That Failed
The tool that promised seamless alignment
After Effects' 'Corner Anchoring' feature sounded like the perfect solution — promising to seamlessly align animations with architectural surfaces. But the result was disastrous: the animation became rigidly locked to four corners, completely losing its dynamism. Instead of helping, this tool turned my crystal ball into a stiff sticker.
Abandoned tracking entirely. Treated the building as the snow globe glass itself. Used Solid Layers with hand-animated Masks to create the sense of light flowing inside crystal — no automated tracking needed.
02
Weeds — The Photo That Broke the Magic
Reality disrupting the dreamed image
The on-site photograph serving as my canvas had weeds in the foreground that severely disrupted the crystal ball's pure, magical aura. The real world kept breaking through the fiction.
Pre-processed the base photograph in image editing software to produce a clean architectural canvas before any compositing began. Reality was edited before the dream could be built on top of it.
Creative Strategy

When high-tech tools
fall short —
create magic with fundamentals.

I stopped trying to make the software do everything for me. Instead of trying to track the building's complex surface, I treated the building itself as the glass of the snow globe.

I used Solid Layers and Masks to mimic light flowing inside crystal. I used the Particle System to create snow falling from the top of the building, drifting outward from its inner depths.

A simple idea, if executed cleverly, has more power than any complicated special effect.
◻
Solid Layers — Base Material

The fundamental building block. Instead of imported footage, solid colour layers became the raw material of light and crystal illusion.

✏
Pen Tool — Carving Knife

Masks hand-drawn with the Pen Tool shaped each layer into architectural surfaces — giving depth to a flat photograph through drawn geometry.

❄
Particle System — Snow Machine

Snow falling from inside the building, not floating in front. Particles emitted from the roofline, drifting downward through the structure.

◈
Screen Blend Mode — The Decisive Move

The "Screen" blending mode removed all black backgrounds, letting only pure light blend with the building. The key detail: snowflakes landing gently on the "S" of the building's logo and resting there for a moment. That one gesture made the whole scene feel alive.

Reflection — The True Purpose of AE

I used AE effectively not by
knowing every function,
but by making it serve my story.

I treated it like a toolbox of basic parts. Each tool had one job, assigned by the story — not by the manual.

The real learning wasn't technical mastery. It was understanding that complex goals are often reached through simple, deliberate choices — not through the most sophisticated tools available.

Results

The final result validates the path of handcrafted design.

What was achieved
  • Water ripples flow naturally across the building's surface — not locked to corners, but organic and alive.
  • Snowflakes drift outward from the building's inner depths, as though the structure contains an entire winter inside.
  • The animation that was once frozen and stiff now blends seamlessly with the architecture.
  • Snowflakes landing on the "S" logo — a micro-detail that makes the whole scene feel inhabited rather than projected.
What was learned
  • The failure of Corner Anchoring was not a setback — it was the lesson that redirected the entire creative approach.
  • Treating the building as the globe's glass (not its surface) was the conceptual breakthrough that made everything possible.
  • Sometimes the simplest path best reaches complex goals. Automation is not always the answer.
  • A tiny true detail — snow landing on a letter — carries more emotional weight than elaborate technical effects.
